# Scenario Analysis & Climate Risks

## Overview
This lecture introduces **scenario analysis** as a tool for navigating deep uncertainty, with a focus on **climate‑related risks**. It distinguishes between **physical risks** (damage from climate events) and **transition risks** (costs of moving to a low‑carbon economy). The lecture demonstrates how to map scenarios to **financial statement impacts** (revenue, costs, capex) and explores how different sectors (green vs. brown) are affected under **early, late, or no action** climate policy scenarios.

---

## Part 1: What Is Scenario Analysis?

### 1.1 Definition
Scenario analysis examines **plausible “what‑if” futures** under specific assumptions. It is **not a forecast**; it is a structured way to think about **pathways** and **uncertainties**.

### 1.2 Why Use It for Climate?
- **Long time horizons** with high uncertainty.
- **Historical data is insufficient**—the future will not look like the past.
- Traditional risk models assume economic structures remain **unchanged**, which is false during a transition.

> [!important] Core Idea
> Scenario analysis helps firms and regulators ask: *What happens to our business if the world takes early climate action, delays action, or does nothing?*

---

## Part 2: Physical vs. Transition Risks

### 2.1 Physical Risks
Arising from the **increasing severity and frequency** of climate and weather events.
- **Examples:** Sea‑level rise, floods, hurricanes, droughts, heatwaves, wildfires.
- **Impacts:** Property damage, supply chain disruption, lower agricultural output, migration, reduced asset values, higher insurance costs.

### 2.2 Transition Risks
Arising from the **adjustment toward a net‑zero emissions economy**.
- **Examples:** Stricter carbon regulations, carbon pricing/taxes, shifts in consumer preferences, technology obsolescence, climate litigation.
- **Impacts:** Reassessment of asset values, change in energy prices, creditworthiness of carbon‑intensive borrowers, but also **opportunities** in green finance and renewables.

> [!tip] The Trade‑Off
> The exact mix of physical and transition risks we face is uncertain, but it is clear that:
> - **No action** → higher physical risks.
> - **Early action** → higher transition risks (in the short term).

---

## Part 3: Why Climate Risk Is Special

| Characteristic | Implication |
|----------------|-------------|
| **Systemic** | Affects entire economies and financial systems, not just individual firms. |
| **Action‑dependent** | Future risk is determined by **policy and technology choices we make today**. |
| **Uncertain yet foreseeable** | We know the direction of travel, but not the exact path or timing. |

## Part 4: Mapping Climate Scenarios to Financial Impacts

### 4.1 The Basic Identity
$$
\text{Net Income} = \text{Revenues} - \text{Direct Emissions Costs} - \text{Indirect Costs} - \text{Capital Expenditures}
$$

### 4.2 How Climate Affects Each Component
- **Revenues:** Changes in demand for products (e.g., fossil fuels vs. renewables).
- **Direct Costs:** Carbon taxes or permit costs on own emissions.
- **Indirect Costs:** Higher input prices passed through from upstream suppliers (e.g., steel, electricity).
- **Capital Expenditures:** Investments in new, cleaner technologies.

> [!note] Example
> A utility company under early action might see **declining revenue** from coal power, **increased direct costs** from carbon pricing, and **higher capex** for wind and solar.

## Part 6: Example Scenario Assumptions (From Lecture)

### 6.1 Impact on Market Return

| Scenario | Mean Market Return | Standard Deviation |
|----------|--------------------|---------------------|
| **Early Action** | 1.4% (Yr1‑10), 1.5% (Yr11‑20), 1.6% (Yr21‑30) | $1 \times$ Historical Std |
| **Late Action** | 1.5% (Yr1‑10), **0.1%** (Yr11‑20), 1.6% (Yr21‑30) | $1.5 \times$ Hist Std (Yr1‑20) |
| **No Additional Action** | 1.4% (Yr1‑20), 1.2% (Yr21‑30) | $2 \times$ Historical Std |

### 6.2 Impact on Gas Price
- Carbon tax increases **expected gas/oil price by 15%**.
- Standard deviation multipliers follow the same pattern as market return.

### 6.3 Physical Risk: Extreme Weather Events
- Baseline annual probability: **10%**.
- After government intervention (carbon tax): **1%**.
- If event occurs, firm profits decrease by **50%**.

## Part 7: Firm‑Level Decisions Under Climate Scenarios

### 7.1 Three Options
1. **Do nothing.**
2. **Infrastructure resilience:** Reduces damage when an extreme weather event occurs.
3. **Greener technology:** Reduces the firm’s sensitivity to gas prices.

### 7.2 Discussion Question
> *Under which scenario is green technology most beneficial?*

**Answer:** **Early Action** or **Late Action** (once transition begins). In these scenarios, gas prices rise (carbon tax) and volatility may increase, so reducing gas‑price sensitivity is valuable. Under **No Additional Action**, physical risks dominate, so **infrastructure resilience** may be more urgent.

## Quick Exam Traps

> [!warning] Trap
> Scenario analysis is **not** about predicting the most likely outcome. It is about **stress‑testing** under different pathways.

> [!warning] Trap
> A firm can face **both** physical and transition risks simultaneously. For example, an oil company faces transition risk from carbon pricing **and** physical risk from hurricanes damaging offshore platforms.

> [!warning] Trap
> “Early Action” does **not** mean all firms benefit. Brown sectors may see immediate negative impacts, while green sectors benefit.
