# Chapter 1 - Introduction to Managerial Accounting

## Core idea

Managerial accounting exists to **help internal managers make decisions**.

Unlike financial accounting, which mainly reports results to outsiders, managerial accounting helps managers answer questions such as:

- How much does this product really cost?
    
- Should we accept a special order?
    
- Which department is efficient?
    
- Are we pricing correctly?
    
- Where are we wasting resources?
    

## 1. Financial accounting vs managerial accounting

### Financial accounting

- Designed for **external users**
    
    - investors
        
    - creditors
        
    - regulators
        
- Uses **formal financial statements**
    
- Follows **GAAP**
    
- Emphasizes:
    
    - objectivity
        
    - reliability
        
    - historical information
        
- Reports are prepared **periodically**
    
    - monthly
        
    - quarterly
        
    - annually
        
- Usually summarizes the company as a whole
    

### Managerial accounting

- Designed for **internal users**
    
    - managers
        
    - employees
        
- Uses **internal reports**
    
    - budgets
        
    - cost reports
        
    - performance evaluations
        
    - decision analyses
        
- Does **not** need to follow GAAP for internal use
    
- Emphasizes:
    
    - relevance
        
    - timeliness
        
    - future orientation
        
- Reports are prepared **as needed**
    
    - even daily or real-time
        
- Highly detailed
    
    - by product
        
    - by customer
        
    - by department
        
    - by region
        

## Memory hook

- **Financial accounting = outsiders + past + rules**
    
- **Managerial accounting = insiders + future + decisions**
    

## 2. Role of managerial accounting in organizations

Managerial accounting supports the **plan–implement–control cycle**.

### Plan

Managers decide what they want to achieve.

- set goals
    
- create budgets
    
- estimate costs
    
- choose strategies
    

### Implement

Managers put the plan into action.

- buy materials
    
- hire labor
    
- produce goods
    
- deliver services
    

### Control

Managers compare actual results with the plan.

- Did costs exceed budget?
    
- Was production efficient?
    
- Did sales meet expectations?
    
- Should corrective action be taken?
    

### Decision making is at the center

Decision making connects all three.  
Managers constantly decide:

- what to do
    
- how to do it
    
- whether it worked
    

## 3. Types of organizations

### Manufacturing company

- buys **raw materials**
    
- converts them into **finished goods**
    
- example: furniture maker, car manufacturer
    

### Merchandising company

- buys **finished goods**
    
- resells them
    
- example: supermarket, clothing retailer
    

### Service company

- provides **services**, not physical products
    
- example: law firm, bank, airline, consulting firm
    

## Important distinction

A **manufacturer makes** goods.  
A **merchandiser sells** goods already made by others.  
A **service company provides actions or expertise**.

## 4. Sustainability accounting and CSR

### Sustainability accounting

Managers use broader information to balance:

- society
    
- economy
    
- environment
    

The idea is not only short-term profit, but **long-term survival and responsible performance**.

### Corporate social responsibility (CSR)

CSR means companies consider the needs of multiple stakeholders, not only shareholders.

Stakeholders may include:

- customers
    
- employees
    
- suppliers
    
- communities
    
- stockholders
    
- environmental and human-rights advocates
    

## 5. Data analytics in managerial accounting

Data analytics = analyzing data with software/systems to draw conclusions.

Managers use it for:

- **descriptive**: what happened?
    
- **diagnostic**: why did it happen?
    
- **predictive**: what will likely happen?
    
- **prescriptive**: what should we do?
    

It is often communicated using:

- graphs
    
- charts
    
- maps
    
- diagrams
    

## 6. Cost classifications

This is a huge exam area.  
A single cost can be classified in multiple ways depending on the question.

---

## 6.1 Out-of-pocket cost vs opportunity cost

### Out-of-pocket cost

A real cash expenditure.

- Example: paying $500 for materials
    

### Opportunity cost

The benefit given up when choosing one alternative over another.

- Example: using factory space for Product A means you cannot use it for Product B
    

## Memory hook

- **Out-of-pocket = money paid**
    
- **Opportunity = benefit sacrificed**
    

---

## 6.2 Direct cost vs indirect cost

### Direct cost

Can be conveniently and physically traced to a cost object.

A **cost object** can be:

- product
    
- job
    
- department
    
- customer
    

Examples:

- wood used in a table
    
- wages of assembly workers on a specific product
    

### Indirect cost

Cannot be conveniently traced to a cost object, so it must be allocated.

Examples:

- factory rent
    
- factory insurance
    
- production supervisor salary
    
- glue or screws used in tiny amounts
    

## Key exam thought

Ask: **Can I trace it directly and conveniently?**

- yes → direct
    
- no → indirect
    

---

## 6.3 Manufacturing cost vs nonmanufacturing cost

### Manufacturing costs

All costs incurred to produce the physical product.  
There are **three basic manufacturing cost categories**:

#### Direct materials (DM)

Major materials that become part of the finished product and can be traced directly.  
Examples:

- wood in a table
    
- steel in a car
    
- leather in a handbag
    

#### Direct labor (DL)

Labor cost that can be physically and conveniently traced to the product.  
Examples:

- assembly-line worker wages
    
- sewing department wages
    

#### Manufacturing overhead (MOH)

All manufacturing costs **other than direct materials and direct labor**.  
Examples:

- indirect materials
    
- indirect labor
    
- factory rent
    
- factory depreciation
    
- factory utilities
    
- maintenance
    
- production supervisor salary
    

### Nonmanufacturing costs

Costs outside the factory.  
Usually split into:

- selling costs
    
- administrative costs
    

Examples:

- sales commission
    
- advertising
    
- office rent
    
- CEO salary
    
- accounting office expenses
    

## Super important rule

If the cost is for **making the product**, it is manufacturing.  
If it is for **selling the product or running the business generally**, it is nonmanufacturing.

---

## 6.4 Product cost vs period cost

### Product cost

A cost attached to units produced.  
For manufacturers, product costs are:

- direct materials
    
- direct labor
    
- manufacturing overhead
    

These go through inventory accounts:

- Raw Materials
    
- Work in Process
    
- Finished Goods
    
- Cost of Goods Sold when sold
    

### Period cost

Expensed in the period incurred.  
Usually nonmanufacturing costs.  
Examples:

- selling expenses
    
- administrative expenses
    

## Memory hook

- **Product cost follows the product**
    
- **Period cost hits expense immediately**
    

---

## 6.5 Variable cost vs fixed cost

### Variable cost

Changes in total as activity changes.  
Example:

- direct materials per unit
    
- hourly labor in some situations
    

If 1 unit needs $5 of material:

- 10 units = $50
    
- 20 units = $100
    

### Fixed cost

Remains constant in total within a relevant range as activity changes.  
Examples:

- factory rent
    
- supervisor salary
    

If monthly rent is $10,000:

- whether you make 1,000 units or 1,500 units, total rent stays $10,000
    

## Important nuance

- **Variable cost per unit** tends to stay constant
    
- **Fixed cost in total** tends to stay constant
    

---

## 6.6 Relevant cost vs irrelevant cost

### Relevant cost

A cost that:

1. differs between alternatives, and
    
2. affects the decision
    

### Irrelevant cost

A cost that does not differ between alternatives, so it should not affect the decision.

### Common irrelevant item: sunk cost

A sunk cost has already been incurred and cannot be changed.  
Ignore it for decision making.

## Decision rule

Only include:

- future costs
    
- future benefits
    
- costs/benefits that differ across alternatives
    

## 7. Ethics and accounting profession

Managerial accountants may face pressure to:

- manipulate numbers
    
- hide cost overruns
    
- overstate performance
    
- misclassify costs
    

Ethical behavior matters because bad accounting information leads to bad decisions.

## Chapter 1 summary box

- Managerial accounting helps **internal decision makers**
    
- Main management cycle: **plan → implement → control**
    
- Three organization types: **manufacturing, merchandising, service**
    
- Three manufacturing costs: **DM, DL, MOH**
    
- Product costs are inventoried; period costs are expensed
    
- Relevant costs are future costs that differ between choices

# Chapter 2 - Job Order Costing

## Core idea

Job order costing is used when each job, project, or customer order is **different**.

Examples:

- custom furniture
    
- construction project
    
- movie production
    
- legal case
    
- yacht renovation
    

Each job gets its **own cost record**.

## 1. Job order costing vs process costing

### Job order costing

Used when:

- many different jobs are produced
    
- each job is unique
    
- costs are accumulated **by job**
    

Main report:

- **job cost sheet**
    

### Process costing

Used when:

- many identical units are produced
    
- production is continuous or repetitive
    
- costs are accumulated **by department/process**
    

Main report:

- **production report**
    

## Memory hook

- **Job order = unique/customized**
    
- **Process = identical/mass-produced**
    

## 2. Manufacturing cost categories in job order costing

Same three as Chapter 1:

- Direct materials
    
- Direct labor
    
- Manufacturing overhead
    

These are assigned to each job.

## 3. How costs are assigned to jobs

### Direct materials

Tracked using a **materials requisition form**.  
This form shows:

- what materials were requested
    
- quantity
    
- cost
    
- which job used them
    

If material can be traced to a job → direct materials on job cost sheet.  
If not → indirect materials go to MOH.

### Direct labor

Tracked using a **labor time ticket**.  
This shows:

- employee name
    
- hours worked
    
- hourly rate
    
- which job the time belongs to
    

If labor can be traced to a job → direct labor on job cost sheet.  
If not → indirect labor goes to MOH.

### Manufacturing overhead

Cannot be directly traced conveniently.  
So it is **applied** using a predetermined overhead rate.

## 4. Job cost sheet

The job cost sheet is the central record for each job.  
It accumulates:

- direct materials
    
- direct labor
    
- applied manufacturing overhead
    

### Formula

```text
Total job cost = Direct materials + Direct labor + Applied overhead
```

If more than one unit is produced in the job:

```text
Unit product cost = Total job cost ÷ Units completed
```

## 5. Predetermined overhead rate (POHR)

### Why do we need POHR?

Because actual overhead:

- is not known until the end of the period
    
- is hard to trace to specific jobs
    
- may fluctuate seasonally
    

So companies use an estimated rate **before** the period begins.

### Formula

```text
POHR = Estimated total manufacturing overhead ÷ Estimated total amount of allocation base
```

Common allocation bases:

- direct labor hours
    
- direct labor dollars
    
- machine hours
    

### Applying overhead to a job

```text
Applied overhead = POHR × Actual amount of allocation base used by the job
```

## Example

If:

- estimated MOH = $120,000
    
- estimated DLH = 30,000
    

Then:

```text
POHR = $120,000 ÷ 30,000 DLH = $4 per DLH
```

If Job A used 500 DLH:

```text
Applied MOH = 500 × $4 = $2,000
```

## 6. Plantwide vs multiple overhead rates

### Plantwide overhead rate

One overhead rate for the whole factory.  
Simple but may be inaccurate.

### Multiple overhead rates

Can use separate rates by department or activity.  
Usually more accurate when different jobs consume resources differently.

## 7. Flow of costs in job order costing

This is extremely testable.

### Step 1: Purchase raw materials

```text
Raw Materials inventory increases
```

### Step 2: Use materials in production

- direct materials → Work in Process (specific jobs)
    
- indirect materials → Manufacturing Overhead
    

### Step 3: Record labor

- direct labor → Work in Process (specific jobs)
    
- indirect labor → Manufacturing Overhead
    

### Step 4: Record other actual overhead

Examples:

- factory rent
    
- utilities
    
- depreciation  
    All go to Manufacturing Overhead
    

### Step 5: Apply overhead to jobs

```text
Work in Process increases
Manufacturing Overhead decreases (applied side)
```

### Step 6: Complete jobs

Completed job cost moves from:

```text
Work in Process → Finished Goods
```

This is **Cost of Goods Manufactured (COGM)**

### Step 7: Sell jobs

When goods are sold:

```text
Finished Goods → Cost of Goods Sold
```

## 8. Cost of Goods Manufactured (COGM)

COGM is the cost of jobs completed during the period.

### Formula

```text
Beginning Work in Process
+ Total manufacturing costs added
= Total cost of work in process
- Ending Work in Process
= Cost of Goods Manufactured
```

Where:

```text
Total manufacturing costs added = Direct materials used + Direct labor + Applied manufacturing overhead
```

## 9. Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

### Formula

```text
Beginning Finished Goods
+ Cost of Goods Manufactured
= Cost of goods available for sale
- Ending Finished Goods
= Cost of Goods Sold
```

## 10. Journal entry logic

You may not need exact account names in every question, but the logic matters.

### Purchase raw materials

```text
Dr Raw Materials
   Cr Accounts Payable / Cash
```

### Direct materials to production

```text
Dr Work in Process
   Cr Raw Materials
```

### Indirect materials to factory overhead

```text
Dr Manufacturing Overhead
   Cr Raw Materials
```

### Direct labor incurred

```text
Dr Work in Process
   Cr Salaries and Wages Payable / Cash
```

### Indirect labor incurred

```text
Dr Manufacturing Overhead
   Cr Salaries and Wages Payable / Cash
```

### Other actual overhead incurred

```text
Dr Manufacturing Overhead
   Cr Various accounts
```

### Overhead applied to jobs

```text
Dr Work in Process
   Cr Manufacturing Overhead
```

### Job completed

```text
Dr Finished Goods
   Cr Work in Process
```

### Sale of goods (cost side only)

```text
Dr Cost of Goods Sold
   Cr Finished Goods
```

## 11. Overapplied vs underapplied overhead

At the end of the period:

- **Actual MOH** usually ≠ **Applied MOH**
    

### Overapplied overhead

Applied > Actual

- too much overhead assigned to jobs
    

### Underapplied overhead

Applied < Actual

- too little overhead assigned to jobs
    

In a simple course setting, the difference is often closed to COGS.

## Quick test mindset

In Manufacturing Overhead account:

- actual overhead is usually on the **debit** side
    
- applied overhead is usually on the **credit** side
    

If debit balance remains → underapplied  
If credit balance remains → overapplied

## Chapter 2 summary box

- Job order costing is for **unique jobs**
    
- Main document: **job cost sheet**
    
- DM uses **materials requisition form**
    
- DL uses **labor time ticket**
    
- MOH is assigned using **POHR**
    
- COGM = completed jobs
    
- COGS = sold jobs

# Chapter 3 - Process Costing

## Core idea

Process costing is used when a company produces **many identical units** through a sequence of departments or processes.

Examples:

- beverage bottling
    
- oil refining
    
- paper manufacturing
    
- chemical processing
    

Instead of tracking each individual job, costs are accumulated **by department**.

## 1. Similarities with job order costing

Both systems:

- assign materials, labor, and overhead to products
    
- compute unit product costs
    
- use Raw Materials, Work in Process, Finished Goods, and Manufacturing Overhead accounts
    
- have similar overall cost flow
    

## 2. Main differences from job order costing

### Job order costing

- many different jobs
    
- costs accumulated by job
    
- unit cost computed by job
    
- major document: job cost sheet
    

### Process costing

- one product or very similar products
    
- costs accumulated by department/process
    
- unit cost computed by department
    
- major document: production report
    

## 3. Cost flow in process costing

Assume two departments: Department A then Department B.

### Direct materials

```text
Raw Materials → WIP Dept. A / WIP Dept. B
```

### Direct labor

```text
Salaries and Wages Payable → WIP Dept. A / WIP Dept. B
```

### Overhead

```text
Manufacturing Overhead → applied to WIP Dept. A / WIP Dept. B
```

### Transfers between departments

When units finish Department A:

```text
WIP Dept. A → WIP Dept. B
```

When units finish final department:

```text
WIP final department → Finished Goods
```

When sold:

```text
Finished Goods → Cost of Goods Sold
```

## Key idea

In process costing, completed units in one department become input for the next department.

## 4. Equivalent units of production (EUP)

This is the hardest idea for beginners.

### Why do we need equivalent units?

At period-end, some units are unfinished.  
We need to express partially completed units as a number of **fully completed equivalent units**.

## Example intuition

Suppose 100 units are 60% complete for conversion cost.  
That means they are equivalent to:

```text
100 × 60% = 60 equivalent units
```

So 100 partially completed units = 60 fully completed equivalent units for that cost category.

## 5. Weighted-average method

The weighted-average method:

- does **not** separate prior-period work from current-period work
    
- blends beginning inventory and current-period costs together
    

### Core rule

Equivalent units under weighted-average are:

```text
Units transferred out
+ Equivalent units in ending WIP
```

## Important note

For process costing, we often separate cost categories because completion percentages may differ:

- direct materials
    
- conversion cost
    

### Conversion cost

```text
Conversion cost = Direct labor + Manufacturing overhead
```

## 6. Production report structure

A process costing production report usually has 4 parts.

---

## Part 1: Physical flow of units

Track where the units went.

### Basic formula

```text
Units to account for = Units accounted for
```

Expanded:

```text
Beginning WIP + Units started = Units transferred out + Ending WIP
```

---

## Part 2: Equivalent units of production

### For weighted-average

```text
EUP for materials = Units transferred out + Ending WIP equivalent units for materials
```

```text
EUP for conversion = Units transferred out + Ending WIP equivalent units for conversion
```

## Example

If:

- 900 units transferred out
    
- 100 units in ending WIP
    
- ending WIP is 100% complete for materials
    
- ending WIP is 40% complete for conversion
    

Then:

```text
EUP materials = 900 + (100 × 100%) = 1,000
EUP conversion = 900 + (100 × 40%) = 940
```

---

## Part 3: Cost per equivalent unit

### Weighted-average formulas

```text
Cost per EUP for materials = (Beginning WIP material cost + Current material cost added) ÷ EUP for materials
```

```text
Cost per EUP for conversion = (Beginning WIP conversion cost + Current conversion cost added) ÷ EUP for conversion
```

Then:

```text
Total cost per EUP = Material cost per EUP + Conversion cost per EUP
```

---

## Part 4: Cost assignment

Assign cost to:

1. units transferred out
    
2. ending WIP
    

### Transferred-out cost

```text
Transferred out cost = Units transferred out × Total cost per EUP
```

### Ending WIP cost

```text
Ending WIP cost = (Ending EUP for materials × material cost per EUP)
+ (Ending EUP for conversion × conversion cost per EUP)
```

## Final check

```text
Total costs to account for = Total costs accounted for
```

## 7. Step-by-step weighted-average procedure

When solving an exam question:

### Step 1

Prepare physical flow of units.

### Step 2

Compute equivalent units for:

- materials
    
- conversion
    

### Step 3

Add costs:

- beginning WIP costs
    
- current costs added
    

### Step 4

Compute cost per equivalent unit.

### Step 5

Assign costs to:

- transferred out
    
- ending WIP
    

### Step 6

Do a total-cost reconciliation.

## 8. Journal entry logic in process costing

### Put materials into production

```text
Dr Work in Process - Dept. A (or B)
   Cr Raw Materials
```

### Record labor

```text
Dr Work in Process - Dept. A (or B)
   Cr Salaries and Wages Payable
```

### Apply overhead

```text
Dr Work in Process - Dept. A (or B)
   Cr Manufacturing Overhead
```

### Transfer from one department to next

```text
Dr Work in Process - Next Dept.
   Cr Work in Process - Prior Dept.
```

### Transfer completed goods to finished goods

```text
Dr Finished Goods
   Cr Work in Process - Final Dept.
```

### Sale of goods

```text
Dr Cost of Goods Sold
   Cr Finished Goods
```

## 9. Common beginner mistakes in process costing

### Mistake 1: forgetting separate completion percentages

Materials and conversion often have different completion levels.

### Mistake 2: forgetting transferred out units are 100% complete

Under weighted-average, transferred-out units count as fully complete for the current department.

### Mistake 3: mixing physical units with equivalent units

Physical units are actual units.  
Equivalent units are partial-completion-adjusted units.

### Mistake 4: forgetting conversion cost = DL + MOH

Very common exam trap.

## Chapter 3 summary box

- Process costing is for **identical units**
    
- Costs are accumulated by **department**
    
- Main report = **production report**
    
- Weighted-average blends beginning and current costs
    
- Use **equivalent units** for incomplete ending WIP
    
- Conversion cost = **DL + MOH**

# Chapter 4 - Activity-Based Costing and Cost Management

## Core idea

Traditional systems often assign overhead using one volume-based driver, such as direct labor hours.  
This can distort product costs when products consume overhead differently.

ABC improves costing by assigning indirect costs based on the **activities** that cause those costs.

## 1. Traditional volume-based costing review

Traditional costing usually uses 3 steps:

### Step 1

Choose one cost driver / allocation base.  
Examples:

- direct labor hours
    
- machine hours
    
- direct labor dollars
    

### Step 2

Compute predetermined overhead rate.

```text
POHR = Estimated total overhead ÷ Estimated total cost driver
```

### Step 3

Assign overhead to products.

```text
Applied overhead = POHR × Actual usage of cost driver
```

## Why this can be a problem

If overhead is caused by many things besides production volume, one single driver may misstate product costs.

## 2. What is Activity-Based Costing (ABC)?

ABC assigns indirect costs to products/services based on the activities they require.

It is designed to improve decision making, especially when:

- overhead is large
    
- products are diverse
    
- complexity differs across products
    

ABC is usually a **supplement**, not a replacement, for the traditional cost system.

## 3. Traditional costing vs ABC

### Traditional costing

- often uses 1 plantwide rate or a few departmental rates
    
- relies mainly on volume-based drivers
    
- simpler
    
- cheaper to operate
    
- less accurate in complex settings
    

### ABC

- uses many activity cost pools
    
- uses multiple cost drivers
    
- includes non-volume drivers
    
- more accurate
    
- more complex and costly
    

## 4. Important ABC terms

### Activity

An event that causes consumption of overhead resources.  
Example:

- machine setup
    
- product testing
    
- inspection
    
- design change
    

### Activity cost pool

A bucket where costs related to a certain activity are accumulated.

### Activity measure / cost driver

The allocation base used to assign the activity pool cost.

## 5. Types of activity measures

### Transaction driver

Counts how many times an activity occurs.  
Examples:

- number of setups
    
- number of inspections
    
- number of purchase orders
    

### Duration driver

Measures the amount of time needed.  
Examples:

- setup hours
    
- processing time
    
- testing hours
    

## 6. Four levels of activity

### Unit-level activity

Performed for each unit produced.  
Varies with number of units.  
Examples:

- installing parts
    
- painting one unit
    

### Batch-level activity

Performed whenever a batch is processed.  
Not driven by number of units inside the batch.  
Examples:

- machine setup
    
- moving a batch
    
- placing an order for a batch
    

### Product-level activity

Supports a specific product line.  
Examples:

- product design
    
- product testing
    
- engineering changes
    

### Facility-level activity

Supports the entire organization.  
Examples:

- plant supervision
    
- factory security
    
- HR
    
- company-wide advertising
    

## Memory hook

- **Unit** = each unit
    
- **Batch** = each batch
    
- **Product** = each product line
    
- **Facility** = whole company/factory
    

## 7. Two-stage allocation in ABC

ABC often works in two stages.

### Stage 1: assign overhead to activity pools

Indirect manufacturing overhead is first distributed into activity pools.

Example pools from the slides:

- engineering and product testing
    
- production setup
    
- machining and assembly
    

### Stage 2: assign activity pool costs to products/services

Then use each activity's cost driver to allocate pool cost to products.

## 8. Activity rates

For each activity pool:

```text
Activity rate = Total cost in activity pool ÷ Total expected activity
```

### Example

If setup cost pool = $300,000 and total setups = 600:

```text
Activity rate = $300,000 ÷ 600 = $500 per setup
```

If Product X requires 40 setups:

```text
Assigned setup cost = 40 × $500 = $20,000
```

Do this for each activity pool, then add them.

## 9. Why ABC can change product costs a lot

A low-volume, complex product may need:

- many setups
    
- more design work
    
- more testing
    
- more inspections
    

Traditional costing may undercost it because it uses only volume.  
ABC captures that extra complexity.

Meanwhile, a high-volume simple product may be overcosted under traditional costing.

## 10. Applying ABC to services

ABC is not only for factories.  
It also works in service companies.

Examples:

- bank: number of transactions, processing time, account openings
    
- hospital: number of procedures, lab tests, patient days
    
- airline: boarding events, baggage handling, maintenance hours
    

## 11. Activity-Based Management (ABM)

ABC measures cost more accurately.  
ABM uses that information to **improve operations**.

Managers ask:

- what activities do we perform?
    
- how much do they cost?
    
- do they add value to the customer?
    

### Goal of ABM

- reduce waste
    
- eliminate non-value-added activities
    
- improve efficiency
    
- improve profitability
    

## 12. ABC limitations

- costly to implement and maintain
    
- may face employee resistance
    
- reports may be misunderstood
    
- does not conform directly to GAAP for external reporting
    
- a company may need two systems
    
    - one for internal ABC analysis
        
    - one for external financial reporting
        

## 13. Characteristics of successful ABC implementation

- strong top management support
    
- linked to evaluation and rewards
    
- cross-functional teams involved
    

## 14. Other cost management methods

### Just-in-Time (JIT) inventory

Objective: reduce inventory and produce only when needed.  
Benefits may include:

- lower storage cost
    
- less waste
    
- faster problem detection
    

### Total Quality Management (TQM)

Continuous improvement of quality.  
Goal:

- prevent defects
    
- reduce rework
    
- improve customer satisfaction
    

### Target costing

Start with market price and desired profit, then derive allowable cost.

```text
Target cost = Expected selling price - Desired profit
```

If expected cost is too high, the firm must redesign or improve operations.

### Life-cycle cost management

Looks at costs over the product's whole life:

- design
    
- development
    
- production
    
- marketing
    
- service/support
    
- disposal/end-of-life
    

## Chapter 4 summary box

- Traditional costing uses mainly **volume-based drivers**
    
- ABC uses **multiple activity cost pools and cost drivers**
    
- Two stages:
    
    1. assign overhead to activity pools
        
    2. assign pool costs to products
        
- Activity levels: **unit, batch, product, facility**
    
- ABC improves cost accuracy; ABM uses that info to improve operations

## Common traps

### Trap 1

Calling factory rent a period cost.

- It is a **manufacturing overhead** cost for a manufacturer.
    

### Trap 2

Forgetting indirect materials and indirect labor belong to MOH.

### Trap 3

Using actual overhead instead of applied overhead in job cost sheet problems.

For process costing, forgetting separate completion percentages for materials and conversion.

For ABC, choosing a poor cost driver.
