

# Should You Mention Retail Investing on Your CV?

The honest answer is: **it depends entirely on how you've done it and how you frame it.**

You're right to be cautious. Your instinct about how bankers might perceive it is partially correct — but not entirely. Let me break down when it helps, when it hurts, and how to handle it.

---

## I. The Banker's Perspective

Here's what's actually going on in the interviewer's head when a student mentions personal investing:

### What They're Worried About

Most students who say "I invest in stocks" fall into one of these categories, and bankers have seen all of them:

| Type | What They Actually Did | How It Comes Across |
|---|---|---|
| **The Meme Trader** | Bought GameStop, AMC, or whatever was trending on Reddit, made or lost money, learned nothing about fundamentals | Cringe. Signals herd behaviour and gambling mentality. |
| **The Index Fund Buyer** | Put money into VOO or QQQ and checked it once a month | Fine, but not interesting enough to mention. Everyone can do this. |
| **The Robinhood Dabbler** | Bought five to ten well-known names (Apple, Tesla, Nvidia) because they use the products or saw the headlines, with no real thesis beyond "I think this company is good" | Not harmful, but not impressive. Banker thinks: "so you bought big tech like every other college student." |
| **The Thoughtful Investor** | Has a clear framework, does fundamental analysis, can articulate a thesis for every position, understands why they bought and what would make them sell, has learned from mistakes | **This is genuinely impressive and worth talking about.** |

The question is which category you fall into. Be brutally honest with yourself.

---

## II. When It Helps You

Mentioning personal investing **adds real value** if you can demonstrate all of the following:

### 1. You Have a Process, Not Just Positions

There's a massive difference between "I bought Nvidia because AI is big" and "I screen for companies trading below fifteen times forward earnings with return on equity above twenty percent, then I read the 10-K to understand the business model before taking a position."

You don't need a sophisticated quantitative system. But you need *some* kind of repeatable process that shows you think before you act.

### 2. You Can Articulate a Thesis for Every Position

If an interviewer points at any stock in your portfolio and asks "why do you own this," you need to be able to give a structured answer:

> "I bought [Company] at $[X] because [thesis — e.g., the market was undervaluing their pricing power in the enterprise segment, which I expected to show up in margin expansion over the next two quarters]. The key catalyst I was watching was [specific event — earnings, product launch, regulatory decision]. The main risk was [specific risk], which I sized my position around. The stock has since moved to $[Y], and [the thesis has played out / I was wrong about Z and here's what I learned]."

That answer demonstrates financial literacy, analytical thinking, risk awareness, and self-reflection. A twenty-year-old who can answer like that is genuinely impressive.

### 3. You've Learned From Losses

This is counterintuitive, but **talking about a losing trade intelligently is more impressive than talking about a winner.** Anyone can talk about a stock that went up — you just sound lucky. Talking about a stock that went down and explaining what you got wrong, what you'd do differently, and how it changed your process — that shows maturity and genuine learning.

> "I took a position in [Company] because I believed [thesis]. What I missed was [what went wrong — e.g., I underestimated how quickly rising rates would compress their multiple, or I didn't account for customer concentration risk that became apparent when their largest client churned]. I lost about [X]% on the position. The lesson I took away was [specific lesson — e.g., I now always check customer concentration before entering a position, or I've become much more disciplined about setting a stop-loss thesis, not just a price target]."

### 4. You Follow Markets with Genuine Depth

If your investing experience means you read earnings transcripts, follow Fed decisions, understand how macro flows through to sector rotation, and can have a real conversation about what's happening in markets — that's a genuine edge in interviews. Many students study finance in theory but don't actually watch markets day to day.

---

## III. When It Hurts You

**Don't mention it if any of the following are true:**

- Your portfolio is entirely meme stocks or highly speculative names and you can't explain the fundamentals
- You've only been investing for a few months and your entire experience is a bull market — you think you're good but you've never been tested
- You trade based on technical analysis alone ("the chart looked like it was breaking out") — this is legitimate in some contexts but most fundamental-oriented bankers will dismiss it
- You can't name the P/E ratio, revenue growth rate, or competitive position of companies you own
- Your portfolio is essentially an index fund with a few extra stocks sprinkled in and there's nothing analytical to discuss
- You've only ever bought stocks and have no understanding of position sizing, risk management, or when to sell

In these cases, it's better to simply not bring it up. The risk of being probed and exposed as superficial outweighs the benefit.

---

## IV. How to Frame It (If You Decide It's Worth Mentioning)

### On Your CV

**Do not put "retail investor" or "personal trading" as a line item on your CV.** You're right that it sounds amateur. Instead, integrate it subtly:

**Option A: Under Interests (Subtle)**

```
Interests: Global macro markets, equity research, jazz saxophone
```

This signals engagement with markets without explicitly saying "I trade stocks." It invites the interviewer to ask follow-up questions, which puts you in control.

**Option B: Under Projects (If You've Done Real Analysis)**

If your investing involves genuine research — say you wrote up detailed investment theses before buying — you could frame one as an equity research project:

```
Independent Equity Research & Investment Analysis             Ongoing
- Manage a personal equity portfolio focused on US-listed companies;
  conduct fundamental analysis including financial statement review,
  valuation (DCF and comps), and competitive positioning assessment
  prior to each investment decision
- Developed and maintained detailed investment theses for [X] positions;
  track thesis progression against quarterly earnings results
```

This only works if it's genuinely true and you can back it up in detail. If you're inflating what you've done, this will backfire badly.

**Option C: Don't Put It on the CV at All — Use It in the Interview**

This is often the smartest approach. Keep your CV focused on concrete, verifiable achievements. Then when the interviewer asks "pitch me a stock" or "what are you following in markets," you draw from your personal investing experience. It comes across as natural and authentic rather than performative.

> "Actually, I own [Company] in my personal portfolio. I bought it at $[X] about [Y] months ago because [thesis]. Let me walk you through my thinking..."

This is powerful because it shows real skin in the game. You didn't just research this for interview prep — you actually put your own money behind your conviction. That carries weight.

## V. The "Pocket Money" Concern

You raised the concern about small amounts. Here's the truth: **nobody cares about the dollar amount.** A senior banker managing a hundred-million-dollar deal is not going to be impressed by the size of a twenty-year-old's portfolio whether it's two thousand dollars or twenty thousand dollars. That's not the point.

What they care about is the **quality of your thinking.** A student who invested five hundred dollars in a company after reading the 10-K, building a simple valuation, and forming a clear thesis is infinitely more impressive than a student who threw ten thousand dollars of family money into Nvidia because everyone was talking about AI.

Never mention dollar amounts. Never talk about returns in percentage terms unless asked directly. Focus entirely on the process and the reasoning.

If you decide to leverage your investing experience, prepare the following before every interview:

**One winner and one loser.** For each, be ready to answer:

| "Why did you buy it?" | Do you have a real thesis or did you follow the crowd? |
| "At what price and what valuation?" | Do you pay attention to valuation or just buy names you like? |

| "What could go wrong?" | Do you understand risk? |
| "What would make you sell?" | Do you have discipline or do you just hold and hope? |

### Know the Macro Backdrop

If you invest in US stocks, you should be able to discuss:
- Where the Fed is in the rate cycle and how that affects equity valuations
- Current market sentiment — are we in a risk-on or risk-off environment?
- Key themes driving the market (AI, reshoring, fiscal policy, whatever is current)
- How the US-China dynamic affects certain sectors or companies you own

### Have a View on Valuation

For every stock you own, know roughly:
- What it trades at on an EV/EBITDA and P/E basis
- How that compares to its peers and its own historical range
- Whether you think the current valuation is justified and why

# From Dabbler to Thoughtful Investor: A Practical Roadmap

This is going to be long because there's no shortcut here. But I'll make it actionable — every section tells you exactly what to do, not just what to read.

---

## I. The Core Problem You're Facing

You said you "find no way" to do serious research. That's not because the information doesn't exist — it's because nobody has told you **what to look for, where to find it, and in what order.** The investment research industry has a massive barrier to entry not because the tools are expensive (most are free) but because the workflow is invisible to outsiders.

Let me make it visible.

---

## II. The Research Process: What Professional Investors Actually Do

Before diving into resources, you need to understand the workflow. Every serious fundamental investor — whether they manage a hundred million dollars or a personal portfolio — follows some version of this process:

```
1. Generate Ideas (where to look)
         ↓
2. Understand the Business (what does it actually do and how does it make money)
         ↓
3. Analyse the Financials (is the business healthy and growing)
         ↓
4. Assess Competitive Position (can it sustain its performance)
         ↓
5. Value the Company (what is it worth vs. what the market charges)
         ↓
6. Identify Catalysts and Risks (what will make the price move, what could go wrong)
         ↓
7. Make the Decision and Size the Position (how much conviction, how much capital)
         ↓
8. Monitor and Re-evaluate (is the thesis playing out)
```

Most dabblers do steps one and seven — they hear about a company and they buy it. Everything in between is missing. That's the gap you need to fill.

---

## III. Step-by-Step Guide

---

### Step 1: Learn to Read Financial Statements (Weeks 1–3)

This is the non-negotiable foundation. You cannot be a thoughtful investor if you can't read an income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement with fluency. You've covered this in Corporate Finance, but there's a difference between understanding the theory and being able to open a real 10-K filing and extract what matters.

**What to do:**

Pick a company you already own. Go to the SEC website (sec.gov), search for the company, and pull up their most recent **10-K** (annual report). Not a summary. Not a Yahoo Finance page. The actual filing.

Now read it. All of it. The first time will be painful and slow. That's normal. Here's what to focus on:

**In the 10-K, the sections that matter most:**

| Section | What You're Looking For |
|---|---|
| **Business Overview (Item 1)** | How does this company actually make money? What are its segments? Who are its customers? What's the competitive landscape? |
| **Risk Factors (Item 1A)** | What does the company itself say could go wrong? These are legally required disclosures and they're surprisingly honest. |
| **MD&A — Management Discussion & Analysis (Item 7)** | This is gold. Management explains what happened in the past year, why revenue grew or shrank, what drove margins, what they expect going forward. Read this section very carefully. |
| **Financial Statements (Item 8)** | The actual income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement. Plus the **notes to the financial statements** — these contain critical details about revenue recognition, debt maturities, stock-based compensation, lease obligations, and accounting policy changes. |

**Your exercise:**

After reading the 10-K, write a one-page summary answering:
1. How does this company make money? (Revenue model)
2. What are the two or three most important drivers of revenue growth?
3. What are the two or three biggest costs?
4. Is the company generating free cash flow? How much?
5. What does the balance sheet look like — is it loaded with debt or sitting on cash?
6. What are the two biggest risks the company faces?

If you can answer all six questions from memory after reading the filing, you understand the business at a level that puts you ahead of ninety percent of retail investors.

**Resources for learning to read financials:**

| Resource | Format | Why It's Good |
|---|---|---|
| **Aswath Damodaran's "Accounting 101" lectures** (YouTube, NYU) | Video, free | The best professor in the world for this. He teaches at NYU Stern and uploads everything for free. Start with his corporate finance course, specifically the sessions on reading financial statements. |
| **Howard Schilit — "Financial Shenanigans"** | Book | Teaches you how companies manipulate their financials. Once you know the tricks, you'll read statements with much more skepticism and insight. |
| **SEC EDGAR Full-Text Search** (efts.sec.gov/LATEST/search-index) | Tool, free | Search any company's actual filings. Get comfortable navigating this. |

### Step 2: Build a Mental Model for Every Business You Own (Ongoing)

The difference between a dabbler and a thoughtful investor is that the thoughtful investor can draw the business on a napkin. You need to understand:

**The Unit Economics:**
- What does the company sell?
- What does it cost to produce or deliver?
- What's the margin per unit?
- How does that scale?

**The Revenue Model:**
- Is revenue recurring (subscriptions, contracts) or transactional (one-time purchases)?
- Is the customer base concentrated or diversified?
- What drives volume growth? What drives pricing power?

**The Competitive Dynamics:**
- Who are the competitors?
- Why would a customer choose this company over alternatives?
- What prevents a competitor from copying this business?

**A practical framework: Porter's Five Forces.** You learned this in business school. Now actually apply it. For every company you own, think through:

1. **Threat of new entrants** — How hard is it to start a competing business?
2. **Bargaining power of suppliers** — Can input costs get squeezed?
3. **Bargaining power of buyers** — Can customers demand lower prices?
4. **Threat of substitutes** — Could customers switch to a completely different solution?
5. **Competitive rivalry** — How intense is competition among existing players?

This takes thirty minutes per company. Write it down. Keep a file.

**Where to learn this kind of thinking:**

| Resource | What It Teaches |
|---|---|
| **Warren Buffett's annual letters to shareholders** (berkshirehathaway.com) | How to think about business quality, competitive moats, and long-term value. Read chronologically from the 1970s onwards. The early letters are shorter and more instructional. Completely free. |
| **Pat Dorsey — "The Little Book That Builds Wealth"** | Specifically about identifying economic moats — competitive advantages that are durable. Short, practical, directly applicable. |
| **Hamilton Helmer — "7 Powers"** | A more rigorous framework for understanding sources of competitive advantage. Slightly more advanced but extremely good. |
| **Michael Mauboussin's research papers** (free on Morgan Stanley or Credit Suisse websites) | The best bridge between academic finance and practical investing. His papers on base rates, ROIC, and competitive strategy are required reading. |

### Step 3: Learn to Analyse the Numbers (Weeks 3–6)

Now that you can read the statements and understand the business, you need to develop fluency in the key metrics that tell you whether a business is healthy, improving, or deteriorating.

**The metrics that matter most:**

| Category | Metric | What It Tells You | How to Calculate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Profitability** | Gross Margin | Pricing power and cost structure | Gross Profit / Revenue |
| | Operating Margin | Operating efficiency | EBIT / Revenue |
| | Net Margin | Bottom-line profitability | Net Income / Revenue |
| | Return on Equity (ROE) | How well the company uses shareholder capital | Net Income / Shareholders' Equity |
| | Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) | How well the company uses *all* capital — **the single most important metric** | NOPAT / Invested Capital |
| **Growth** | Revenue Growth (YoY) | Top-line momentum | (Revenue this year - last year) / last year |
| | Earnings Growth (YoY) | Bottom-line momentum | Same formula for EPS or net income |
| | Same-store sales / organic growth | Growth excluding acquisitions and new units | Usually disclosed by management |
| **Financial Health** | Net Debt / EBITDA | Leverage — how many years of earnings to pay off debt | (Total Debt - Cash) / EBITDA |
| | Interest Coverage | Can the company service its debt comfortably | EBIT / Interest Expense |
| | Current Ratio | Short-term liquidity | Current Assets / Current Liabilities |
| | Free Cash Flow Conversion | Does reported earnings actually turn into cash | FCF / Net Income |
| **Efficiency** | Asset Turnover | Revenue generated per dollar of assets | Revenue / Total Assets |
| | Days Sales Outstanding | How quickly customers pay | (Receivables / Revenue) × 365 |
| | Inventory Turnover | How quickly inventory sells | COGS / Average Inventory |

**The exercise:**

For every company you own, build a simple spreadsheet with five years of historical data for these metrics. You can pull the raw data from the 10-K filings or from free sources like Macrotrends or SimFin.

Then look at the **trends.** A single year's number tells you very little. Five years of trends tell you everything:
- Are margins expanding or compressing? Why?
- Is revenue growth accelerating or decelerating? Why?
- Is the company generating more or less free cash flow relative to earnings? Why?
- Is leverage going up or down? Why?

The "why" is always more important than the "what." Numbers are symptoms. Business dynamics are the disease or the cure.

**The most important metric most retail investors ignore: ROIC.**

Return on invested capital tells you whether the company is actually creating value or just growing for the sake of growing. A company that grows revenue at 20% but earns 5% ROIC is destroying value — it's investing capital at returns below its cost of capital. A company that grows at 8% but earns 25% ROIC is a compounding machine. This single concept will change how you evaluate businesses.

**Where to learn this:**

| Resource | What It Teaches |
|---|---|
| **Aswath Damodaran — "The Little Book of Valuation"** | Concise, practical introduction to valuation for non-experts. |
| **McKinsey — "Valuation: Measuring and Managing the Value of Companies"** | The bible. Dense but comprehensive. Use it as a reference, not a cover-to-cover read at this stage. |
| **Damodaran's spreadsheets** (pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar) | He publishes his models, datasets, and valuation templates for free. Download them, study them, replicate them. |

### Step 4: Learn Valuation — What Is the Stock Actually Worth? (Weeks 4–8)

This is where your Corporate Finance coursework and the financial modelling project we discussed earlier directly apply. The key shift in mindset is:

**Price is what you pay. Value is what you get.**

A great company at the wrong price is a bad investment. A mediocre company at a great price can be a good investment. Most dabblers buy companies they admire without asking whether the current price already reflects everything good about the business.

**The three valuation lenses you need:**

**A. Multiples (Quick Sanity Check)**

You already know these from comps. For personal investing:

| Multiple | Quick Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **P/E** | How many years of current earnings you're paying for. A P/E of 25 means you're paying 25 years of earnings upfront. Is that justified by growth? |
| **EV/EBITDA** | Same concept but at the enterprise level, stripping out capital structure and depreciation differences. |
| **PEG Ratio** (P/E ÷ Earnings Growth Rate) | Adjusts P/E for growth. A PEG below 1 *might* indicate undervaluation. Rough rule of thumb, not gospel. |
| **FCF Yield** (FCF / Market Cap) | The inverse of price-to-FCF. Tells you the cash return on your investment at the current price. A 5% FCF yield means the company generates 5 cents of free cash for every dollar of market cap. |

Always compare multiples to:
1. The company's own historical range (is it trading above or below its five-year average?)
2. Peers in the same industry
3. The growth rate (a high P/E is justified if growth is high; a low P/E might be a value trap if growth is declining)

**B. DCF (Intrinsic Value Estimate)**

You're building this skill through Project A. For personal investing, you don't need a full-blown model for every stock. A simplified back-of-the-envelope DCF works:

> "This company generates $5 billion in free cash flow. If FCF grows at 10% for 5 years and then 3% in perpetuity, and I discount at 10%, the present value is roughly $X. The current market cap is $Y. So I'm paying [more/less] than what I think the business is worth."

The point is not precision. The point is having a framework for asking "is the price roughly right, clearly cheap, or clearly expensive."

**C. Reverse DCF (The Most Underused Tool)**

Instead of projecting cash flows to estimate value, you take the current stock price and **work backwards** to figure out what growth rate the market is implicitly assuming.

> "The stock trades at $150. For that price to be justified at a 10% discount rate, the market is implying 18% annual FCF growth for the next ten years. Is that realistic? The company has grown at 12% historically. So either the market is too optimistic, or I'm missing a growth driver."

This is extraordinarily powerful because it turns valuation into a **probabilistic judgment** rather than a prediction. You're no longer saying "I think the stock is worth $X." You're saying "the market is pricing in Y% growth, and I think the probability of achieving that is high/low."

### Step 5: Follow Earnings and Management Commentary (Ongoing)

This is where you transition from researching backwards to watching the story unfold in real time.

**Every quarter, for every company you own:**

1. **Read the earnings press release** — released before the earnings call. Contains the key numbers.
2. **Listen to or read the earnings call transcript** — this is where management explains the numbers and answers analyst questions. The Q&A section is often more informative than the prepared remarks because analysts push on weak spots.
3. **Compare actual results to your expectations** — Did revenue come in above or below what you expected? Did margins behave as you predicted? If not, why?
4. **Update your thesis** — Is your reason for owning this stock still intact? Has anything changed?

**Where to get earnings call transcripts for free:**
- **Seeking Alpha** (free tier gives you access to transcripts)
- **The Motley Fool** (publishes transcripts)
- **Company investor relations websites** (often post audio and transcripts directly)

**The habit that separates serious investors from dabblers:**

Before every earnings report, write down in one or two sentences what you expect and what you're watching for. After the report, write down what actually happened and whether your thesis changed. This takes five minutes and it builds a track record of your own thinking over time. Six months from now, you can look back and see patterns in where your judgment was right and where it was wrong.

### Step 6: Develop a Sell Discipline (Critical and Often Ignored)

Most retail investors — including most dabblers — have no framework for selling. They either panic-sell during drawdowns or hold forever because they're emotionally attached. Both are wrong.

**You should sell when:**

| Reason | Example |
|---|---|
| **The thesis is broken** | You bought because you expected margin expansion from a new product line. Two quarters in, the product is failing and margins are compressing. The reason you bought no longer holds. |
| **The valuation has become extreme** | The stock has tripled and now trades at 50× earnings with 10% growth. The risk-reward has shifted dramatically against you even though the business is fine. |
| **You find something better** | Your capital is limited. If a new opportunity offers clearly better risk-adjusted returns, it's rational to swap. This requires comparing across your portfolio. |
| **You were wrong about the fundamentals** | You misread the competitive dynamics, the customer concentration risk materialised, or management turned out to be less competent than you thought. |

**You should NOT sell when:**

| Temptation | Why It's Wrong |
|---|---|
| The stock dropped 15% on no news | Price volatility is not the same as business deterioration. If nothing has changed about the fundamentals, a lower price means better value. |
| Everyone on social media is negative | Sentiment is not analysis. |
| You're bored | Not a reason. |
| You want to lock in gains | If the thesis is intact and the valuation is still reasonable, selling just because you're up is cutting your winners short. |

## IV. The Books That Will Actually Change How You Think

Not a reading list for the sake of a reading list. These are the books that, in my honest assessment, create the biggest shifts in how you approach investing. Read them in this order:

| Order | Book | What It Does To Your Brain |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **"The Most Important Thing" — Howard Marks** | Teaches you that investing is about risk management, not return chasing. Completely reframes how you think about uncertainty, market cycles, and second-level thinking. Short, readable, essential. |
| **2** | **"One Up on Wall Street" — Peter Lynch** | Teaches you to invest in what you understand and to look for opportunities in your everyday life. Grounds investing in common sense rather than complexity. |
| **3** | **"The Little Book That Builds Wealth" — Pat Dorsey** | Teaches you to identify durable competitive advantages. After reading this, you'll never buy a stock without asking "what's the moat?" |
| **4** | **"Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits" — Philip Fisher** | Teaches you qualitative research — how to evaluate management, growth potential, and business quality through investigation, not just numbers. The concept of "scuttlebutt" research originated here. |
| **5** | **"The Intelligent Investor" — Benjamin Graham** (read the chapters on Mr. Market and Margin of Safety, skip the rest or read the Jason Zweig annotated version) | The philosophical foundation of value investing. The Mr. Market metaphor will permanently change how you relate to stock price movements. |
| **6** | **"Thinking, Fast and Slow" — Daniel Kahneman** | Not an investing book, but the single most important book for understanding the cognitive biases that cause investment mistakes. Overconfidence, anchoring, loss aversion — they're all in here. |
| **7** | **"You Can Be a Stock Market Genius" — Joel Greenblatt** (ignore the terrible title) | Teaches you to look for opportunities in special situations — spinoffs, restructurings, mergers — where information asymmetry creates mispricing. More advanced, but fascinating. |

- [ ] Write the one-page summary (six questions from Step 1)
- [ ] Build the five-year historical metrics spreadsheet (Step 3)
- [ ] Start reading Matt Levine's Money Stuff daily

### Week 3–4: Valuation

- [ ] Run a simplified DCF on that same company (even a back-of-the-envelope one)
- [ ] Do a reverse DCF: what growth rate is the current stock price implying?
- [ ] Pull up three to five comparable companies and compare multiples
- [ ] Form a written thesis: Buy, Hold, or Sell, with a rough price target and the key assumptions behind it
- [ ] Start reading Howard Marks' "The Most Important Thing"

### Week 5–6: Process Building

- [ ] Set up an earnings calendar for companies you own
- [ ] Before the next earnings report, write down your expectations
- [ ] After the report, listen to the earnings call, read the transcript, and write down what surprised you
- [ ] Evaluate your thesis: has anything changed?
- [ ] Read Pat Dorsey's "The Little Book That Builds Wealth"

### Week 7–8: Expand

- [ ] Pick a second company — ideally one you're considering buying but haven't yet
- [ ] Run the full process: 10-K → business understanding → financial analysis → valuation → thesis
- [ ] Write it up as a one-to-two page investment memo
- [ ] Start reading Buffett's annual letters (start from the 1970s, read five per week)

### Ongoing

- [ ] Keep a written investment journal — just a few sentences per trade: why you bought, what you're watching, what would make you sell
- [ ] Review your journal quarterly: where was your judgment right? Where was it wrong? What patterns do you see?
- [ ] Gradually read through the book list
- [ ] Listen to Acquired and Odd Lots during commutes

## VI. One Final Piece of Advice

The gap between a dabbler and a thoughtful investor is not talent, intelligence, or access to information. It's **discipline and consistency.** The tools are all free. The filings are public. The frameworks are well-documented. What separates the people who actually become good at this from the people who stay dabblers forever is whether they do the work — repeatedly, honestly, even when it's tedious.

Reading one 10-K is hard and slow. Reading your tenth is fast and natural. Building your first DCF takes a week. Building your fifth takes an afternoon. Writing your first investment thesis feels clumsy. Writing your tenth feels effortless.

The compound interest metaphor applies to skills, not just money. Start now, stay consistent, and in a year you'll be the person in the interview room who can talk about markets with genuine depth — not because you memorised answers, but because you've been doing the work.
