# Interview Prep Based on Your CV: Likely Questions & How to Answer Them

I've gone through your CV carefully. Below are the questions you're most likely to face, organized by category, with practical answer guidance tailored specifically to your background.

---

## I. "Walk Me Through Your Resume"

This is almost guaranteed to be the opening question. They want a concise, structured narrative — not a chronological reading of your CV.

**How to answer:**

> "I'm Jermaine, a first-year BBA student at HKUST studying Information Systems, with a 3.57 GPA and Dean's List in my first semester. I chose this major because I wanted to combine a strong business and finance foundation with technical skills — I believe that's increasingly where the industry is heading.
>
> Outside the classroom, I've been actively seeking hands-on experience. Earlier this year, my team won the HKSTP Venture Capital Investment Challenge, where I built a full investment proposal including valuation work and presented to a VC panel — that experience really solidified my interest in the analytical side of finance. Before that, I led a team in the Alvarez & Marsal case competition, where I got exposure to go-to-market strategy work.
>
> On the technical side, I'm comfortable with financial modelling — DCF, LBO, and comps — and I work regularly with Excel, Python, and Bloomberg Terminal. I've also completed the Bloomberg Market Concepts certification.
>
> I'm here today because I want to apply these skills in a real deal environment and learn from experienced professionals. Investment banking is where I see the sharpest intersection of analytical rigour and commercial impact, and that's what excites me."

**Key principles:**
- Present → Past → Future structure
- Under two minutes
- End with *why this role*, not just *who you are*
- Don't just list items — connect them into a story

### "Tell me about your relevant coursework — what did you actually learn in Corporate Finance / Derivatives?"

They're checking whether you absorbed the material or just sat through lectures.

> **Corporate Finance:** "We covered the core framework for how firms make financial decisions — capital budgeting using NPV and IRR, the trade-offs in capital structure between debt and equity, the Modigliani-Miller propositions, and dividend policy. The most useful concept for me was understanding how WACC ties together the cost of debt and equity to evaluate investments — it connects directly to DCF valuation."

> **Derivatives:** "We studied options, forwards, futures, and swaps — both the pricing mechanics and, more importantly, how they're actually used for hedging and risk management. For example, understanding put-call parity and how you can synthetically replicate positions gave me an intuition for how risk gets transferred in markets. In a banking context, this is relevant because so many structured products and hedging strategies are built on these fundamentals."

## III. Questions About Your Experiences

### "Tell me about the HKSTP VC Investment Challenge. What company did you evaluate? Walk me through your analysis."

This is your strongest differentiator. They'll dig deep here. Be specific.

> "We were tasked with acting as a VC fund evaluating a potential investment. My team analysed [Company Name / industry — fill in the actual details]. We built the investment thesis around three pillars: the market opportunity, the company's competitive moat, and the management team's ability to execute.
>
> For valuation, we used a combination of comparable company multiples and a DCF. Given that this was an earlier-stage company, the DCF required more assumptions about the growth trajectory, so we relied more heavily on comps to triangulate. We also built out a risk mitigation framework covering key risks like customer concentration and regulatory change, and proposed an exit strategy — [IPO / trade sale / secondary — fill in the actual detail].
>
> We presented to a panel of practicing VC investors, and they pushed back hard on our assumptions around [specific thing — e.g., revenue growth rate, market size]. That taught me the importance of being able to defend your assumptions with data rather than just asserting them."

**Preparation tip:** You absolutely need to remember the specific numbers, the company, the pushback you received, and what you learned. Vague answers here will hurt you.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "What multiple did you use and why?"
- "What was the biggest risk and how did you mitigate it?"
- "If you could redo the analysis, what would you change?"
- "What did the judges disagree with?"

### "Tell me about the A&M case competition. What was your approach as team leader?"

They care less about the case content and more about your leadership and problem-solving process.

> "We were given a brief to develop a five-year go-to-market strategy for an advanced tax compliance software targeting the Asia-Pacific region. As team leader, I structured our approach into three workstreams: market sizing and prioritisation, competitive positioning, and the financial plan.
>
> I assigned each team member based on their strengths — one person on market research, one on the competitive landscape, and one on financials — while I handled the overall narrative and the final presentation. We also built a software mockup in Canva and Tableau to make our proposal tangible rather than purely conceptual.
>
> The biggest challenge was managing different opinions on which markets to prioritise. Two team members wanted to go broad across Southeast Asia, while I believed we should focus on two to three high-value markets first to build credibility. We resolved it by running a simple scoring framework weighing market size, regulatory complexity, and competitive intensity. That data-driven approach got everyone aligned."

### "You work as a Library Helper. Why is that on your CV?"

Don't be defensive. Frame it as showing work ethic and interpersonal skills.

> "Honestly, I included it because it shows two things. First, I'm someone who's willing to work — I manage my time between academics, extracurriculars, and a part-time role. Second, it's given me real experience handling high-volume, fast-paced interactions. I'm dealing with a thousand-plus students and staff daily, troubleshooting IT issues on the spot, and coordinating with full-time staff. It's not glamorous, but the ability to stay calm, be helpful under pressure, and solve problems quickly is relevant in any professional setting."

## IV. Technical Skills Deep-Dive

### "You list Financial Modelling — DCF, LBO, Comps. Walk me through a DCF."

Use the detailed framework from our earlier conversation. This is your moment to prove the technical skills on your CV are real.

### "You say Advanced Excel with VBA. Give me an example of something you've built."

You need a concrete answer here. If you've built anything — a model, a macro, an automation — describe it. For example:

> "I built a VBA macro that automates the sensitivity analysis in a DCF model. Instead of manually changing WACC and terminal growth rate inputs and recording each output, the macro iterates through a range of values for both assumptions and populates a two-dimensional data table automatically. It also formats the output with conditional formatting to highlight the base case. It saved significant time when I was working on the VC challenge valuation."

If you haven't built something like this yet, build one before your interviews. They will ask.

### "How would you use Python in a banking context?"

> "The most immediate use case is data processing. If I need to pull financial data for twenty comparable companies, clean it, and calculate trading multiples, doing that manually in Excel is slow and error-prone. In Python, with pandas and NumPy, I can automate that in minutes and rerun it whenever the data updates.
>
> I've also used Python for tasks like scraping public filings, running regression analyses for beta estimation, and building simple screening tools to filter a universe of companies by financial characteristics. In a junior banking role, I think the biggest value-add is being able to automate the repetitive data work so the team can focus on the actual analysis and judgment."

### "You have Bloomberg Terminal experience and the BMC certification. What do you actually use Bloomberg for?"

> "I use it primarily for pulling comparable company data — trading multiples, historical financials, and market data. The functions I'm most comfortable with are EQ for equity screening, FA for financial analysis, COMP for comparable company analysis, and GP for charting. I've also used it for fixed income data and economic indicator tracking.
>
> The BMC certification covered the four modules — economic indicators, currencies, fixed income, and equities — and gave me a framework for how professional investors interpret market data. For example, understanding how central bank actions feed through to yield curves and then to equity valuations."

## V. Market / Commercial Awareness Questions

Given your stated interest in macro and equity markets, expect these.

### "What's happening in markets right now? What are you following?"

You need to have a current, specific answer. General framework:

> "The three things I'm watching most closely right now are [fill in with what's actually happening when you interview — for example]:
>
> First, [a macro theme — e.g., the rate environment: where central banks are in the cycle, what the Fed / HKMA / PBoC is doing and why it matters for deal activity].
>
> Second, [a sector or deal — e.g., a recent IPO, M&A transaction, or market event in Asia that's relevant to the bank you're interviewing at].
>
> Third, [a longer-term trend — e.g., AI's impact on financial services, regulatory changes in Hong Kong, capital flows between mainland China and Hong Kong]."

**Critical:** Read the Financial Times, Bloomberg, or WSJ daily for at least two weeks before your interview. Have two or three specific, well-formed views. They don't need to be contrarian — they need to be informed and coherent.

### "Pitch me a stock."

Have one prepared. Structure:

> "I'd recommend looking at [Company]. It trades at [current price] with a market cap of [X].
>
> **The thesis in one sentence:** [Why this stock will go up / is undervalued].
>
> **Three supporting points:**
> 1. [Business quality / competitive advantage]
> 2. [Catalyst — what will make the market re-rate it: earnings growth, new product, regulatory tailwind]
> 3. [Valuation — it trades at X times earnings versus peers at Y times, and I think that discount is unjustified because...]
>
> **Key risk:** [The main thing that could go wrong and why I think the risk-reward still favours the long side]."

Pick a company you genuinely understand. It's better to pitch a simple, well-reasoned idea on a company you know than a flashy pick you can't defend.

## VI. Behavioural / "Fit" Questions

### "Why investment banking?"

> "Three reasons. First, the analytical rigour — I genuinely enjoy building models and working through the logic of how a business creates value. The VC challenge was the first time I did that in a competitive, time-pressured environment, and I found it energising rather than draining.
>
> Second, the exposure. As a junior banker, you work across industries, across deal types, and alongside senior people who've been doing this for decades. There's no faster learning curve in finance.
>
> Third, the impact. Banking sits at the centre of how companies grow — whether through raising capital, acquiring other businesses, or restructuring. I want to be in the room where those decisions get shaped."

### "Why this bank specifically?"

Research the bank before every interview. Your answer must include:
1. Something specific about their recent deal activity (cite a deal)
2. Something about their platform that matches your interests (e.g., strong Asia coverage, sector focus)
3. Something about culture or people (ideally from networking conversations)

### "Why Hong Kong?"

Given your background (Guangzhou → HKUST), this is natural:

> "Hong Kong is uniquely positioned as the bridge between mainland China and international capital markets. For someone like me — Mandarin-native, Cantonese-fluent, English-proficient, and studying at HKUST — there's no better place to start a career in finance. The concentration of deal flow, the regulatory environment, and the access to both Chinese and global markets is unmatched in Asia."

### "What's your biggest weakness?"

Pick something real but manageable, and show self-awareness:

> "I tend to be overly detail-oriented — I'll spend extra time perfecting the formatting of a model or presentation when the substance is already there. I've gotten better at recognising when something is 'good enough' and moving on, especially after the case competitions where time pressure forced me to prioritise. But it's something I'm actively working on."

### "Tell me about a time you faced conflict in a team."

Use the A&M case competition — the market prioritisation disagreement:

> Structure: **Situation → Task → Action → Result (STAR)**
>
> "During the A&M competition, two teammates wanted to target all of Southeast Asia while I believed we should focus on two to three markets. Rather than just overruling them as team leader, I suggested we build a simple scoring framework together — market size, regulatory complexity, competitive intensity. Once we scored each market objectively, the data pointed clearly toward focusing on Singapore and Australia first. Everyone bought in because the process was fair and transparent. We ended up with a much stronger, more defensible proposal."

## VIII. Questions to Ask Them

Always have three to four prepared. Good ones for your profile:

1. "What does the deal pipeline look like right now, and what sectors are you seeing the most activity in?"
2. "What distinguishes the interns who get return offers from those who don't?"
3. "How is the team structured day-to-day — how much interaction do interns get with senior bankers?"
4. "I come from an Information Systems background with strong technical skills. Have you seen analysts use Python or automation tools on the job, and is that something the team values?"

Avoid asking about hours, pay, or anything you can Google.
